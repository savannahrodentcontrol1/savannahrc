Savannah Rat & Mouse Removal: What Locals Get Wrong

A practical field guide for coastal Georgia homeowners — how to recognize a real infestation, what professionals actually do that DIY can't, and how to choose a company that fixes the problem instead of just billing for it.

Published 2025 · Reading time 7 min

Most Savannah homeowners discover their rodent problem the same way: a strange scratching sound in the wall at 2 a.m., a chewed bag of dog food, or a single dropping behind the toaster. By the time those signs show up, the population has usually been quietly growing for weeks. This playbook walks you through what's actually happening inside your walls, what to do about it, and how to tell a good rodent specialist from a forgettable one.

Section One

Why coastal Georgia is rodent territory

Savannah's climate is a near-perfect environment for rats and mice. Winters rarely drop cold enough to shut down breeding, the humidity supports the moisture rodents need, and the city's older housing stock — particularly in the Historic District, Ardsley Park, and Thomas Square — provides countless entry points through aging soffits, crawl spaces, and original wood framing.

The region also has natural rodent corridors. The Savannah River, marshlands, drainage infrastructure, and dense oak canopies all give rodents constant travel routes between properties. Once one home becomes hospitable, neighboring homes are typically next. The University of Georgia Extension Service has documented rodent activity patterns specific to Georgia counties, and Chatham County consistently ranks among the higher-pressure areas in the state.

A single pair of mice can produce 30+ offspring in six months. Time is never on the homeowner's side.

Section Two

Where they actually hide in your home

Most people picture rodents skittering across kitchen floors. In reality, kitchens are just feeding grounds — the nests are usually somewhere else entirely.

Attics are the single most common nesting zone in Savannah homes, especially for roof rats, which climb effortlessly along tree limbs and utility lines. Wall voids are next, followed by crawl spaces and finally the living areas — which most people assume is the primary problem zone, but is actually where rodents only travel to feed.

Section Three

The signs that come before you see a rodent

By the time you actually see a live rodent in daylight, the population has usually been established for weeks or months. The earlier warning signs are easier to catch if you know what to look for:

Pellet-shaped droppings

Dark, rice-sized droppings along baseboards, behind appliances, in cabinets, or in the attic. Fresh droppings are dark and soft; older ones are gray and crumble.

Gnaw marks on unexpected surfaces

Wood trim, electrical wire insulation, plastic food containers, even soap. Rodents need to chew constantly to keep their incisors filed down.

Nighttime scratching or scurrying

Rodents are mostly nocturnal. If you're hearing activity in walls or ceilings between dusk and dawn, that's the population telling you it's there.

Greasy rub marks along walls

Rodents follow the same paths repeatedly. Their fur leaves dark, oily smudges on baseboards, pipes, and the bottoms of door frames.

An unexplained odor

A persistent musty or ammonia-like smell — often strongest near walls or in basements — is a sign of urine accumulation from an established population.

Section Four

The health side most homeowners overlook

Rodent damage to property is easy to picture. The health risk is the part that catches people off guard. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, rodents are directly or indirectly linked to more than three dozen diseases, ranging from salmonellosis and leptospirosis to hantavirus. Even without direct contact, the dander, urine, and droppings rodents leave behind become airborne particles that aggravate asthma and allergies — especially in children and older adults.

This is why proper post-treatment cleanup matters. Killing the rodents only solves part of the problem; the contamination they leave behind is the actual health hazard, and it needs to be cleaned with proper protective equipment and disinfectants.

Killing the visible rodents is the easy part. The real work is sealing the home so the next ones can't get in.

Section Five

Choosing the right specialist

Most pest control companies offer rodent service as a sideline to their main insect business. A handful specialize in it. The difference shows up in results — specialists tend to focus on the entry-point sealing work that determines whether the problem actually stays solved, while generalists tend to set traps, collect a check, and move on to the next house.

When evaluating providers, ask whether they perform a written property inspection before quoting, whether they include exclusion work (sealing entry points) as part of their service, and whether they offer follow-up visits. The answers will tell you most of what you need to know. For Savannah-area homeowners looking for a dedicated specialist, rodent control services in Savannah from a local provider who handles the full inspection-removal-exclusion workflow is generally a better fit than a general pest company.

Homeowner Prevention Checklist

  • Seal every exterior gap larger than a quarter inch with steel wool, hardware cloth, or proper sealant — not expanding foam.
  • Trim tree limbs back at least three feet from the roofline; oaks and palms in Savannah are common rodent highways.
  • Store pet food, bird seed, and grass seed in sealed metal or hard-plastic containers.
  • Empty outdoor trash bins regularly and keep lids tightly closed; rinse bins monthly to remove food residue.
  • Fix water leaks promptly — rodents need water sources and will travel toward them.
  • Schedule a professional perimeter inspection annually, ideally in early fall before peak migration indoors.

FAQ

Common questions from Savannah homeowners

Q · 01How long does a typical rodent removal take?

Most cases are resolved within two to four weeks, including follow-up visits. Heavier or older infestations involving structural sealing can take longer, but you should see a sharp drop in activity within the first week.

Q · 02Is winter the worst season for rodents in Savannah?

Activity does spike in late fall as rodents move indoors for warmth, but Savannah's mild climate means infestations happen year-round. Summer outdoor populations often translate into fall indoor problems.

Q · 03Are traps and bait stations safe around pets?

A licensed professional will use tamper-resistant placement that keeps treatments away from pets and children. Consumer-grade bait used carelessly is one of the more common sources of accidental pet poisoning.

Q · 04Will one treatment solve the problem permanently?

Removal alone won't. The reason rodents come back is that the entry points are still open. A complete service includes exclusion — physically sealing the gaps — which is what makes the result last.

Q · 05What should I do before the inspector arrives?

Clear access to attics, crawl spaces, and behind major appliances. Write down everywhere you've seen droppings, gnaw marks, or heard noises. Pet owners should crate animals during the inspection.
